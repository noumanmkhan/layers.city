# Layers.City

A city is divided up in a dozen ways at once: official neighbourhoods, council wards, state or provincial and federal electoral districts, business districts, the informal names locals actually use, and the transit lines that tie it together. Each of those lives in a different dataset on a different website. Layers.City puts them on one interactive map you can switch on and off, then tap any spot (or search an address or place) to see every boundary it falls inside, what it's like to live there and who represents it. It's built for two kinds of people: a resident learning things they probably didn't know about their own city, and a newcomer getting their bearings.

The maps share one engine, with a folder of data and settings per city:

- **Toronto:** https://layers.city/toronto/
- **Chicago:** https://layers.city/chicago/

## Toronto

An interactive map of the City of Toronto where you turn civic and cultural boundaries on and off, then tap any spot to see every boundary it falls inside.

![Layers.City Toronto: the whole city with former cities, highways and rapid transit](assets/screenshot.png)

### Why

Toronto's geography is described in several overlapping ways at once. A single address can be in Old Toronto, "Downtown", the official neighbourhood of Wellington Place, what everyone calls King West, the Toronto Downtown West BIA, Ward 10, the provincial riding of Spadina—Fort York and the federal riding of Spadina—Harbourfront. Each of those lives in a different dataset on a different website. No public site I could find shows them together on one simple map.

**Universities, colleges and hospitals:** universities and colleges are one toggle in the Education group, *Colleges & universities*, with a chip for each to show either or both; hospitals are a toggle under Landmarks (all off when the map opens). They show how these are laid out across the city. They follow fixed rules rather than listing everything: universities are public or nonprofit, one point per campus; colleges are public only, so no private career colleges and no public-private partnership campuses (a public college's name on a campus run by a private company, whose students Ontario cut off from post-graduation work permits in 2024); hospitals are Ontario public hospitals plus OHIP-funded ones like Shouldice, with a heavier ring for an emergency department. Chicago applies the same rules from the federal IPEDS and CMS directories: universities with 1,000 or more students, the seven City Colleges, and public or nonprofit hospitals with an ER (two Prime Healthcare hospitals that turned for-profit in 2025 are left out). Names only: no ratings, rankings or wait times.

**Schools (Toronto):** one toggle in Education puts every publicly funded school on the map: 817 schools of the TDSB, TCDSB, Viamonde and MonAvenir, from the Ontario Ministry of Education's school list (2024–25, Open Government Licence – Ontario), fetched by GitHub Actions twice a year. Chips pick Elementary (kindergarten to grade 8) or Secondary (grades 9 to 12) or both, and a second row narrows to French-language schools (Viamonde and MonAvenir, taught in French, mainly for children with a francophone parent) or French immersion schools (174 TDSB and TCDSB schools teaching mostly in French to children from English-speaking homes, from the boards' own 2026–27 lists, matched to the Ministry's schools by address). Icons appear from zoom 13. Tap a school for its grades (JK–5, JK–8, 6–8, 9–12…), board, address and website. The card's Nearby section counts elementary and secondary schools within 1 km. It never names a "nearest school": in Toronto the board, not distance, decides which school a home is zoned for, and no board publishes its attendance areas as open data. Only the descriptive fields are kept: the Ministry file's test results and student figures never reach the repo, so nothing is ranked. Private schools are left out.

**Libraries & community centres (every city):** the first toggle in *Community & Faith* (in Dallas, *Community*) puts the public libraries and community centres on the map, the same places the What's here card's Nearby section already names: Toronto Public Library branches and City-run recreation centres, Chicago's libraries, Park District fieldhouses and Community Service Centers, Vancouver's library branches and Park Board centres, Dallas's libraries and recreation centers. A chip each for libraries and centres. With about 300 points in Toronto and Chicago, the icons appear from zoom 12 only, so the city view stays clear. `engine/community.py` writes them from the Nearby points each time a city's `nearby.py` runs.

**Community & Faith (Toronto and Chicago):** a newcomer often wants to live near a mosque, gurdwara, synagogue or temple, and the shops their household relies on. Two toggles, both off when the map opens. *Places of worship* covers churches, mosques, Hindu temples, synagogues, Buddhist temples, gurdwaras and other faiths, with a chip for each faith to show any mix; *Halal & kosher food* covers halal grocers and butchers and kosher grocers, butchers and bakeries, with a chip for each. One toggle per need rather than nine keeps the panel short, and the chips still let someone see only the places that matter to them. Places of worship come from OpenStreetMap with hand-kept fixes, because OSM misses storefront congregations and new buildings (the Islamic Society of Toronto's masjid, opened in 2025, was still pinned at its old site). Shops come from the certifiers' own lists, HMA for halal and COR for kosher: independents only, each shop once with all its roles, no chain-store counters or restaurants. Churches, about 900 of them, get a second row of chips while their chip is on, to show one family at a time (Catholic, Anglican, Baptist, United, Presbyterian, Orthodox, Evangelical, Other). While a faith or shop is showing, the card's Nearby section names the nearest one. The rule: map facilities, never people. Nothing labels or shades a neighbourhood by who lives there. Chicago has the same two toggles: its churches inside the city (about 2,300) with families fitted to it (Catholic, Baptist, Methodist with the AME churches, Lutheran, Episcopal, Presbyterian, Orthodox, Evangelical, Other); its mosques, synagogues, temples and gurdwaras across Chicagoland, since most gurdwaras and Hindu temples are in the suburbs; halal stores from Halal Monitoring Services' list and kosher grocers and bakeries from the cRc's.

**Skyscrapers:** a toggle under Landmarks (off when the map opens) places every building 150 m and taller: 117 in Toronto (plus 13 under construction) and 138 in Chicago. Each badge shows what the tower is for (homes as a grid of windows, offices and hotels as floor bands, mixed use as both), stays hollow until the building is open for occupancy (topping out isn't enough), and gets a gold ring at 300 m and up, the supertalls: SkyTower and One Bloor West in Toronto, Willis, Trump, St. Regis, Aon, the Hancock, Franklin Center and Two Prudential in Chicago. Tap one and the readout gives its height, floors and year with a few facts: where it ranks in the city, whether it was once the city's tallest, the tallest of its decade or its kind, and a handful of written-up records (Willis was the world's tallest building for almost 25 years; First Canadian Place was Canada's tallest for half a century). The list comes from Wikipedia's tallest-buildings pages, refreshed monthly; this year's openings are checked by hand before a tower turns solid.

**Landmarks:** a short, curated list of the places Torontonians give directions by (Scotiabank Arena, High Park, Massey Hall, City Hall, Exhibition Place…), not an exhaustive points-of-interest database. Big, spread-out anchors appear city-wide; dense downtown venues appear as you zoom in, and overlapping labels are hidden automatically.

**Address search:** type an address (for example "789 Yonge St") and the map flies there, drops a pin and fills in the readout below. Addresses are matched with OpenStreetMap's Nominatim service across the GTA, with Toronto matches listed first; an address beyond the city limits switches the map to the regional view. Landmarks and place names also suggest themselves as you type, without any network call: landmarks with their old names and nicknames (SkyDome, Air Canada Centre, Ryerson, "the Ex"), plus every neighbourhood, known-as name, former city and surrounding municipality. Choosing a place like Deer Park or Mississauga frames it on the map and fills in the readout, switching to the regional view for places outside the city. Beyond Toronto, the GTA's named communities suggest themselves too (Meadowvale, Port Credit, Woodbridge, Unionville, Brooklin, Kleinburg…, about 470 in all, from OpenStreetMap's place names): choosing one shows where it is, with its municipality and region, and an address in the GTA gets a "Near Meadowvale"-style pill. Most GTA municipalities don't publish neighbourhood boundaries the way Toronto does, so these are named points for finding your way, not drawn areas. On the Chicago map the same works for community areas, the sides and suburbs like Lombard.

**Shareable links:** the address bar keeps up with the map as you use it: the pin and its name, the layers that are on, the lens and any "Narrow it down" picks, the 416 or 416 + 905 view, and where the map is looking. Copy it, or use the Share button (in the readout, and at the foot of the layer panel; on phones it opens the share sheet), and whoever opens the link sees the same map. Everything sits after the `#`, so it never reaches a server, and anything a link leaves out falls back to the starting setting. Layers are named by short ids that don't change when a layer's label does, so old links keep working. Works the same on the Chicago map.

**Save as an image:** the Image button at the foot of the layer panel saves the map as a 3840 × 2160 PNG, with or without its labels. It's made entirely in the browser: the map is drawn from the site's own shapes rather than third-party tiles, so it can be redrawn in a 16:9 frame covering what's on screen and turned into a picture with [html-to-image](https://github.com/bubkoo/html-to-image), loaded only when first used. The panels, pin and controls are left out, and a small credit line with the map's address and its data sources is stamped in the corner, as the data licences ask. On phones it opens the share sheet.

**More room for the map:** on a computer, the arrow at the top of the layer panel folds it away to a small Layers button; the choice is remembered. On phones the panel already folds down to its title.

**Heritage districts:** a spot inside a protected district gets a line on the readout saying which one and the year it was designated ("Cabbagetown South (designated 2005)"); elsewhere the line doesn't appear. In Toronto these are the Heritage Conservation Districts in force under the Ontario Heritage Act; districts still under study or under appeal aren't shown, so the map never presents them as protected. Chicago's equivalent is its Landmark Districts.

**Around the city:** zoom out to see every municipality in the Greater Toronto Area (Halton, Peel, York and Durham regions) as plain grey shapes, for context.

**416 or 416 + 905:** a switch at the top of the layer panel picks the extent. *Toronto* (the default) greys out everything beyond the city limits and clips the GO lines at the boundary, leaving the TTC in full; *Greater Toronto Area* shows the highways and GO lines across the region.

**Starting view and Reset:** the map opens on Toronto with neighbourhoods and their badges, landmarks, main streets, highways, GO and the subway switched on, and everything else off: a deliberately quiet first view, so former cities, Old Toronto areas, known-as names and streetcars are each one click away rather than all on at once. *Reset*, at the bottom of the layer panel, returns to that view from wherever you've wandered (it also clears any neighbourhood filter); *Hide all* turns every layer off.

**Toronto in the GTA:** the highways continue past the city limits (OpenStreetMap data, joined to the City's centrelines at the boundary) and the GO Train lines run out to Barrie, Kitchener, Niagara Falls and Oshawa. Lines that share track near Union fan out as you zoom in. Tapping a spot outside Toronto names its municipality and region.

**Neighbourhood lens:** for someone new to Toronto, a quick sense of what each neighbourhood is like to live in. One lens at a time shades the 158 neighbourhoods in three plain tiers rather than precise figures:

- *Housing cost:* lower, middle or higher, from median home value and median rent, each ranked against the rest of the city and weighted by how many households own or rent.
- *Getting to work:* mostly transit, walk or bike; mixed; or mostly car, from the share of commuters who drive.
- *Renters and owners:* mostly owners, a mix, or mostly renters.
- *To Union:* the typical transit trip to Union Station on a weekday morning: under 30 minutes, 30–45, 45–60, or over an hour. Computed ahead of time from the TTC, GO and UP Express schedules with [r5py](https://r5py.readthedocs.io/), from points about 400 m apart across each neighbourhood (about 4,000 in all), leaving any time between 8 and 9 am. Walking to the stop and waiting count. Each neighbourhood shows the median of its points.
- *Housing type:* pick a type (detached, semi and row, low-rise apartments or towers of five storeys and up) and the neighbourhoods are shaded by its share of their homes: under 10%, 10–30%, 30–60%, or 60% and more.

**Suburban lens:** in the Greater Toronto Area view, each municipality is shaded by its typical drive to Union Station with no traffic: under 30 minutes, 30–60, 60–90, or 90 and over. A grid of points about 1.5 km apart covers the suburbs (about 2,900 in the GTA); each is timed with [OSRM](https://project-osrm.org/) on OpenStreetMap roads at posted speeds, and each municipality shows the median of its points. Tapping a spot outside the city adds the time from there, so someone in a big municipality like Caledon sees their own area, not an average. The times are best case on purpose: they compare places, and anyone can add their own rush hour.

**Use my location:** on a phone, the target button beside Find pins where you are and opens the card for that spot. Everything is worked out in the browser, so your position never leaves the phone, and it's kept out of shared links.

**Images for sharing:** the Image button saves the map as a picture in four shapes: wide 4K, or square, portrait and story sizes for Instagram. Focused on a neighbourhood, the picture frames it and carries its name as a title, which makes a ready-made post.

**Heritage buildings:** a toggle beside Heritage districts marks every building protected on its own: Toronto's 1,515 properties designated under Part IV of the Ontario Heritage Act (from the City's Heritage Register; listed properties are left out) and Chicago's 412 individually designated Chicago Landmarks. Each is a small diamond, shown once you zoom in to street level (and named closer still); tap one for the year it was built, its architect and when it was designated, and the What's here card adds a *Heritage building* row when you're standing on one. Named ones (Robie House, the King Edward Hotel) are in search.

**Neighbourhood badges (Toronto, in review):** a toggle under Neighbourhoods, on when the map opens, lays a faint one-colour drawing inside a few neighbourhoods, of what the place is known for: the CN Tower and Rogers Centre in Harbourfront-CityPlace, the mosque and slab towers of Thorncliffe Park, the Leuty Lifeguard Station in the Beaches. Public buildings and landscape only, never logos or anyone's artwork. The drawings turn to run parallel to Steeles Avenue, like the city's street grid, and a build step finds the largest box of each one's shape, turned the same way, that fits inside the neighbourhood, so a drawing never spills into its neighbours.

**Bike lanes:** a toggle under Getting around draws each city's bike network from its own open data, Toronto's Cycling Network and Chicago's Bike Routes, protected lanes in lime and painted ones in a darker olive green, yellow-greens that don't read as Line 2 or the rivers. Chips sort the lanes by how separated they are from traffic, in any mix: *Protected* (cycle tracks and protected lanes), *Painted* (painted, buffered and contraflow lanes), *Shared* (sharrows, signed routes, greenways) and, in Toronto, *Trails*. Protected and Painted show when the layer first opens. Tap a stretch for its type, the streets it runs between and the year it went in. The layer shows lanes as built; it can't say whether construction has one closed today, because neither city's permit data says which lane a permit closes. In Toronto, *Bike Share stations* sits under it: every Bike Share Toronto station as a small dot from zoom 12, filled where it charges e-bikes, with its number of docks and whether the kiosk takes cards. The card's Transit nearby section names the nearest station within 500 m. It leaves out live counts of bikes and free docks, which change by the minute. A GitHub Action refreshes the networks and the station list weekly.

**Parking:** a Parking group with three layers. *Green P parking* draws the City's car parks as small squares (filled for a garage, outlined for a surface lot, bigger for more spaces); tap one for its spaces, clearance and whether it's a TTC commuter lot. The City's open list stops in 2019, so the layer says so and leaves out rates. *Permit parking areas* outlines the 97 areas where residents can buy an on-street permit, labelled by code; the card's Permit parking row names the spot's area and how much of its neighbourhood lies inside permit areas, and a *Permit parking* lens shades every neighbourhood by that share (almost all in the old city and its inner suburbs). *EV chargers* shows public charging from Natural Resources Canada's station listings (U.S. AFDC data) plus the City's own chargers: filled dots with a bolt for DC fast, ringed for Level 2 only, bigger for more ports; tap for ports by level and power, network, hours and price as listed. Chips narrow each points layer by type. A GitHub Action refreshes all three monthly.

**All, Basic and Live:** the layer panel has three tabs. Basic is the city as it stands, data that changes slowly (census figures, boundaries, landmarks, routes). Live is the city as it's changing this week, refreshed by GitHub Actions from city feeds: road closures daily, What's changing weekly, with each row saying when its data was last updated (in red if a refresh has been missed). The tabs only filter the list; what's on stays on.

**Directions:** the map deals in straight-line distances, so every place the card names (the spot itself, the voting place, the nearest library, community centre and emergency department) has a directions button that opens Google Maps, or on Apple devices a choice of Apple Maps or Google Maps, for the real route and travel time from wherever you are.

**Collection day and street sweeping:** the card answers the most local question there is. In Toronto it gives the spot's garbage day and what goes out on the next two pickups (green bin and recycling one week, garbage the next), from the City's collection areas and its yearly calendar; a building with its own collection gets told so. Chicago publishes no garbage schedule, so its card gives the next street sweeping dates for the ward section instead, April to November, which is the one that costs you a ticket.

**Road closures on the card:** tap or search a spot and the What's here card lists any transit-construction closures or lane restrictions within about a five-minute walk, with the hours and the permit dates, whether or not the closures layer is on. "They closed my street for the subway, how long for?" gets its answer without hunting for the red line.

**Special events:** big events close roads in waves, and the City publishes every one of those closures in the same Road Restrictions feed, flagged as a special event and filed under the organizer. A toggle under Getting around (in the All and Live tabs) draws them in one violet, so a race course reads as a single line: solid where the road is closed now, lighter where it closes later that day, gone once it reopens. Tap a stretch for its exact times and the organizer's closure notice. For a big event (the Waterfront Marathon, say), the map does more for the few days around it: a banner on someone's first visit ("TCS Toronto Waterfront Marathon is on this weekend") opens a view of its own, also listed first under Views, that turns everything off except main and collector streets, highways and the event's closures, and frames the course. Dismissing the banner or taking the view is remembered, so it doesn't nag. Events are named from a short list of organizers, or else from the closure text; nothing is drawn by hand, so next year's marathon, a parade or a festival shows up on its own once the City files its closures. When the feed and the police's own closure notice disagree, a short list of corrections retimes or drops the feed's closures and adds the stretches it missed, traced along the City's street centrelines; those closures say in their popup that the times come from the police. Where the organizer publishes its course (the Waterfront Marathon's official GPX), the layer also draws it as a thin line over the closures, with a mark at every kilometre (every fifth when zoomed out) and start and finish flags, until the race is over. In New York the marathon's closures don't come from the permit feed but from the City's own closure notice, a PDF posted the week before; a daily job reads it and traces each closed stretch along the street centerline, and since the organizer publishes no course file, no course line is drawn.

**New development:** a toggle (*New development*, in the What's changing group) draws the big changes around the city: new buildings that aren't single houses, demolitions of bigger buildings and, in Toronto, rezoning, site plan and subdivision applications. Each site is a dot coloured by where it is: proposed, approved, under construction or finished in the last two years (demolitions are dashed rings), with chips to show any mix. Tap one for what it is, its dates and the City's application page; the What's here card adds a *Changing nearby* section listing what's within about a five-minute walk. Toronto's come from the City's development applications and building permits (permits placed on the City's address points); Chicago publishes building permits but no proposals, so it shows approved and finished work. A weekly GitHub Action refreshes both. Proposals are labelled as proposals, not decisions.

**Colour by party:** switch on provincial or federal ridings (in Chicago and Dallas, state legislative or congressional districts) and each seat is shaded in its sitting member's party colour: red for Liberal, blue for Conservative, orange for NDP, green for Green in Canada; blue for Democrat and red for Republican in the US; grey for a vacant seat. The riding lines turn to a dark line on a white halo so borders still show in a sea of one colour. One riding layer is shaded at a time (the most recent wins), and it stands in for the Former cities colours and the lens, which come back when the shading is switched off. City wards stay uncoloured: Toronto and Chicago elect councillors without party labels.

**Election view (Toronto, until November 15, 2026):** switch on City wards and each ward is shaded in one neutral violet, chosen because no party uses it, with the other shading switched off. Ward numbers become bold chips, and zoomed in each one shows the election date, the sitting councillor (marked if not running again) and how many candidates are on the ballot. Tap a ward and the card opens on Representatives, with the candidates listed under the councillor. A councillor running somewhere else says so ("running for mayor", "running in Ward 14") rather than just "not running". The mayoral race is citywide, so it isn't on the ward labels: a Mayor row heads every card's Representatives with all 53 candidates in ballot order, and the election view's note points to it. No candidate is singled out; choosing frontrunners would be a political judgment the map shouldn't make.

**November 3 ballot (Chicago and Dallas, until election day):** tap anywhere and, under each state representative, state senator and U.S. representative, the card lists that district's candidates with their party, in the order they appear on the ballot, and says whether the sitting member is running again, not running, or running for something else. U.S. Senate and Governor follow. Chicago's come from the Board of Elections' candidate list; Dallas's from Dallas County's official sample ballot, which is published only as a picture, so it is read with OCR and checked by eye. The city councils come later: Chicago's ward races in February 2027 and Dallas's council in May 2027 will get Toronto's election view once their ballots are set. It all comes from the City's election results file, so on election night the labels switch to the count and then the winner. The parties come from the weekly representatives check, so a by-election or a floor-crossing shows up within a week. Dallas's congressional districts stay uncoloured until January, because the members sitting now were elected on the old lines.

**Parks:** an *Outdoors* group holds a *Parks* layer, off by default: every City park outline from the City's Green Spaces data, shaded green, named on hover. Chips narrow it to parks with a *Playground*, *Water play* (a splash pad or wading pool), a *Washroom* or an *Off-leash* area; pick several and a park needs all of them, while the rest fade to a faint wash so the green space still reads. Tap a park for what it has and its toronto.ca page. The amenities come from the City's parks list, matched to the outlines once at build time; the City's list names no splash pads, so those come from OpenStreetMap. A GitHub Action refreshes it all monthly.

**Panel groups:** the toggles come in groups, in the order most people need them: Places, Outdoors (Toronto), Getting around, What's changing (the live layers: new development, transit under construction, road closures and special events), Education, Parking & Charging (Toronto), Representation, Community & Faith (libraries and community centres, places of worship, halal and kosher food), and Lenses (the neighbourhood and suburban lenses, which shade the map rather than add to it). Every city uses the same order and skips the groups it has nothing for, and the All, Basic and Live tabs keep it too. A tap on a group's heading folds it away, so someone who never needs ridings doesn't scroll past them. A folded group says how many of its layers are on, and the browser remembers which are folded.

**Views:** a dropdown at the top of the panel sets up the map for a job in one pick: Explore downtown (landmarks, towers, heritage, the subway, streetcars and GO, framed on downtown), Know your city (every neighbourhood coloured by its transit trip to Union, a way into the other lenses), Who represents me (one set of boundaries at a time, leading with whatever the next election is for, and a prompt to enter your address), Where to live (housing cost with frequent transit) and Getting around (every rapid and frequent line). Each view replaces whatever was on; change anything and the dropdown reads Custom. The views are plain config in each city's `city.json`, and links keep them.

**A guided first visit:** first-time visitors get a small invitation ("New to Toronto? Take a one-minute tour"), and a Tour button in the panel footer starts it any time. Five steps per city teach the city through the map: each one switches on just the layers it talks about, outlines them in the panel so you learn where they are, and frames the right part of the map. Toronto: the six former cities, Downtown and its neighbours, East or West of Yonge, getting around, and tap anywhere. Chicago: the nine sides, the 77 community areas, the address grid, the 'L' and Metra, and tap anywhere. When it ends (Done, ×, or Escape) the map goes back to exactly how it was; the arrow keys step through it. The text lives in each city's `city.json`, so it can be edited without touching code.

**How addresses work:** a toggle under Main streets explains each city's addresses. In Chicago (*Address grid*) State and Madison are drawn as the zero lines and every mile line is dashed and numbered (800 N, 1600 N, 2400 N…; Halsted 800 W, Ashland 1600 W…), and the What's here card reads a spot's numbers off the grid ("About 2400 N · 1200 W"). Toronto has no grid: *Yonge: East and West* draws Yonge and tints the streets it splits, East in teal and West in orange, and the card says which side a spot is on with a nearby example ("East of Yonge… Wellesley St E"), plus how numbers run.

**Compare two places:** *Compare with another place* on the What's here card, then tap the map or search, and the card widens into one table: every row of the card (boundaries, Living here, schools, transit nearby, Nearby, representatives) with its label above the two places' values, A on the left and B on the right, pinned on the map in red and teal. It works across the city limits too (Cabbagetown against Square One). Nothing is marked better or worse; it only lines the descriptions up. Tap elsewhere to swap the second place, × to drop either one, and a shared link keeps both.

**Focus on a place:** tap a neighbourhood's or area's name on the map (Cabbagetown, Downtown, Scarborough), or *Focus on* in the What's here card, and everything outside it dims. Subway, train and bus lines that pass through stay bright inside and half-faded beyond it, so you can follow each one to where it ends; the rest fade away. The panel lists what runs through the place: lines, the streetcar and bus routes with a stop inside (tap a number to draw the route), the main streets in or along it, and the landmarks, campuses, hospitals and skyscrapers inside. Tap outside it or press Escape to leave; a shared link keeps the focus.

**Focus on a region:** in the Greater Toronto Area view, pick Halton, Peel, York or Durham (or type its name in search). The map outlines it, dims everything else, labels its municipalities and lists them in the panel with their drive times; tap one to go there. Tapping a spot outside the city also offers "Show all of" its region, and a shared link keeps the focus.

**Narrow it down:** under the lens legend, pick what you're looking for (say, middle housing cost and up to 45 minutes to Union) and the neighbourhoods that don't fit fade out. A *Most common home* row picks by each place's commonest home type (detached, semi and row, low-rise or towers; in Chicago detached, attached, 2–19 units or 20+), so every neighbourhood falls under exactly one. The ones that do are listed by name; tap one to fly there. It answers the question a newcomer actually asks: "where should I be looking?"

**What stands out:** under the card's pills, one line says what makes the neighbourhood stand out among its peers, such as "North St. James Town: highest share of renters in Toronto (89% rent)" or "Englemount-Lawrence: among the quickest transit trips to Union in North York". A build step (`engine/facts.py`) ranks every neighbourhood against the whole city and against its own former city and Old Toronto area, on figures the map already has: housing cost, renters and owners, getting to work, home types, age of homes and time to Union. A fact needs a top-3 place and a figure clearly away from the group's middle, so about three in four neighbourhoods get one and the rest show none rather than a forced one. Language and other demographic figures never feed it, and affordable areas are described as "among the most affordable", never "cheapest". Chicago's community areas get the same, compared with the city and their side.

The What's here card adds a *Living here* section with the same tiers and the figures behind them (median home value and rent, time to Union, how people get to work, share of households renting). The point is the feel of a place relative to the rest of Toronto, not a price list.

Three more rows describe the place without ranking it: *Home types* (the mix of detached, semi and row, low-rise and tower homes), *Built* (when its homes went up: 1960 or before, 1961–80, 1981–2000, 2001–21) and *Language at home* (the top three languages people speak most often at home; the second and third show only at 5% or more, so a 1% language never reads as a feature of the place). Language is description only: it never shades the map, filters a shortlist or feeds a ranking. On the Chicago map the same rows come from the American Community Survey: buildings by number of units (detached, attached, 2–19 units, 20 or more), year built (1939 or earlier through 2000 and later), and language at home in the Census's 12 broad groups, which is all it publishes for areas this small. A *Tree canopy* row and lens show how much of each area's land is under trees, from ESA WorldCover 2021, a 10 m satellite land-cover map read in a GitHub Action; its citywide figures (30% for Toronto, 16% for Chicago) match the cities' own canopy studies, and the leafiest places get a "what stands out" fact ("Among the leafiest in Etobicoke"). Two more rows follow: *Ages* (a stacked bar: under 15, 15–24, 25–44, 45–64, 65 and over) and *Recent immigrants* (in Toronto the share who came to Canada in 2016–21, from the 2021 Census; in Chicago the share born abroad who came to the US in 2010 or later, from the ACS, a longer window, which the card says). Like language, both are description only.

**Moving between maps:** the map's name at the top of the layer panel is a menu. Pick another city to jump straight to its map, or *All maps* to return to the maps hub; an *All maps* link above the name does the same on wider screens. Each city's menu is built from the cities in the repo, so a new city appears in every map's menu on its next build.

**Light or dark:** the page follows your device's setting, or you can pick Light or Dark at the bottom of the layer panel. The rest of the site (the hub, About, the guides, Compare and the venue pages) follows the same setting and the choice made on the map; only the fact sheets stay light, because they're made to print.

**What's here:** click anywhere and a card sums up the spot in a few coloured pills, then breaks it down in sections you can open and close (the page remembers which you keep open):

- *Boundaries:* former city, area, neighbourhood, nearby known-as names, BIA, ward, provincial riding, federal riding and the spot's ward on each of the four school boards.
- *Living here:* the neighbourhood lens tiers and the figures behind them.
- *Schools (Chicago):* the Chicago Public Schools whose attendance area the spot is in: the elementary school, the middle school where one has its own area (22 do), and the high school, each with its grades, the straight-line distance to the building and a link to its CPS profile. From CPS's 2025–26 boundaries on the City's Data Portal. Names and grades only: no ratings or test scores.
- *Nearby:* the nearest library branch, community centre and emergency department, with the distance, and how many parks, playgrounds, parks with tennis courts and dog off-leash areas are within 1 km (about a 15-minute walk). Counts only, nothing scored. From the City's parks and recreation file and Toronto Public Library; Chicago's uses the Chicago Public Library, the Park District's fieldhouses and the City's community service centers (community centres count only if publicly run and open to everyone) and OpenStreetMap, and works in miles: distances in miles (feet when very close) and counts within half a mile, about a 10-minute walk.
- *Where to vote:* around an election (Toronto's October 26 municipal vote; Chicago's November 3 general election, where the card lists the nearest of the Board of Elections' vote centers, which any Chicago voter can use, early and on election day, and names the ward and precinct with a link to the Board's lookup for the precinct's own polling place, which the Board doesn't publish), a box under the pills says where people living at that spot vote on election day: the building, its address and distance, the entrance to use, a button that shows the spot and the voting place together, and a link to the City's MyVote, which has the final word. It comes from the City's voting subdivisions (each a small slice of a ward with one voting place), checked daily in October, and disappears when the polls close.
- *Representatives:* the mayor, then the city councillor, MPP and MP for that spot, each name linking to their official page on toronto.ca, ola.org or ourcommons.ca, with party for the MPP and MP (Toronto councillors run without party labels). Until November 15, 2026 the councillor row also lists the ward's council candidates under a caret. Then a school trustee row for each board: before the October 26, 2026 election it lists the candidates; once the City's results file has a winner it names them, marked as taking office November 15; from then on, just the trustee.

The boundary lookup runs in the browser with point-in-polygon tests against every layer, whether or not the layer is switched on.

**School board wards:** in April 2026 Ontario capped school boards at 12 trustees, so the TDSB's 22 wards became 12 and the City's open trustee-ward file (2018 lines) went out of date. The City Clerk's reference chart defines every 2026 trustee ward, for all four boards, as a group of whole City wards, so the map builds them by merging City wards rather than waiting for a new file. Which school a given address is zoned for isn't shown for Toronto: none of the boards publishes its attendance boundaries as open data. Chicago's map does show it, because CPS publishes its attendance areas every school year.

**Keeping representatives current:** a GitHub Action runs every Monday, reads the current members straight from the City, the Legislative Assembly and the House of Commons, and republishes the site only if something changed, so by-elections and the new council after an election show up within a week. Names come from the same pages they link to. Open North's representatives data was tried first and dropped: it still listed an MP six months after her by-election.

**Transit under construction and road closures:** lines being built are drawn dashed in their future colour, and nested under them is the one layer on the map that changes every day: road closures and lane restrictions caused by that construction. A GitHub Action runs just after midnight Eastern, reads Toronto's live Road Restrictions feed (permits filed by Metrolinx and the consortiums building its lines) and Chicago's CDOT permits (those naming the Red Line Extension or the Red/Purple rebuild), ties each permit to the nearest line being built or upgraded, and republishes. Arterials always show; full closures of side streets appear when zoomed in, since that's where most actual closures are (the streets crossing a new line). Tapping one gives the hours and the permit dates, labelled as permit dates: permits get padded and renewed, so they aren't a forecast of when a road reopens.

**Streetcars and buses:** there are far too many bus routes to draw at once (230 in Toronto, 124 in Chicago), so the map sorts them into families you can mix (frequent, express, regular, or all of them; overnight on its own) and draws any single route on demand. The families come from the schedule itself rather than the agency's branding: *Frequent* means a trip every 10 minutes or better each way through a weekday from 7 am to 7 pm (Toronto's 43 such bus routes read as a grid across the city, with 10 of the 11 streetcar routes on their own layer); *Express* is what the agency calls express, dashed when shown with the other families; *Overnight* means at least an hourly trip each way between 2 and 4 am, which in Toronto is the Blue Night network. Toronto's 11 streetcar routes get a layer of their own, in red, numbered along the line. Route numbers follow each agency's own style: in Toronto, streetcars red, buses white with a red outline, express green and Blue Night blue, as on TTC signs; in Chicago, local buses dark grey, express red, X routes green and Owl blue, from the CTA's bus map legend. Frequent routes are drawn with a heavier line. So bus lines on smaller streets don't float in space, a *Collector streets* toggle under Main streets draws the next tier of roads from zoom 13: with it on, 93% of Toronto's bus network and 88% of Chicago's sits on a drawn street, rather than 82% and 68% (expressways bring both to 94%). The readout gains a *Transit nearby* section: the nearest subway or 'L' station, every route with a stop within about a 5-minute walk, the overnight routes, and the nearest stop. Frequent routes have a double ring; tap any number there (or search "29" or "Dufferin") and that route is drawn in ink with its stops. On the map, tapping a route's line or its number badge shows it alone, with everything else faded, until the next tap elsewhere. GO buses have a toggle of their own under GO Transit trains: the whole regional network in the GTA view, and in the city view just the routes that stop in Toronto (most run nonstop on the highways and stop only at terminals such as Union, Yorkdale, York Mills, Finch and Scarborough Centre, or along Yonge north of Sheppard). They're numbered "GO 41" so they never mix with the TTC's 41, show up in Transit nearby, and can be searched and drawn like any other route. Chicago's equivalent is *Pace buses* under Metra: the suburban network in the Chicagoland view and the 49 routes that stop in the city (Pulse Milwaukee and Dempster, Harlem, Cicero, Touhy, Howard…) in the city view, with a Pace row in Transit nearby. Pace offers its GTFS for non-commercial use only, so these come from OpenStreetMap's route relations instead: routes and named stops, but no timetable, so Pace routes show no frequency. A GitHub Action reads the TTC's, the CTA's and GO's GTFS each week, keeps each route's main shapes, counts trips per hour, and the build sorts routes into families from those counts. Nothing is ranked: how often a bus comes is described, never scored.

## How it's built

- **One engine, one folder per city.** The map page is a shared engine (`engine/`) that knows how to draw kinds of layer: filled areas, official neighbourhoods, representation boundaries, business areas, streets, highways, regional rail and rapid transit. Everything about Toronto lives in `cities/toronto/`: `city.json` holds the layer list, labels, lenses and the rows of the "What's here" card, and `city.css` holds Toronto's colours. Adding a city means adding its data and config, not changing the engine.
- **Front end:** one static HTML page using [Leaflet](https://leafletjs.com/), with no framework. `engine/assemble.py` inlines the city's config and colours into the engine page, so each city's map is a single file. Colours come from CSS variables, so the map follows the viewer's light or dark setting. The layout works on phones.
- **Data pipeline** (`cities/toronto/`): Python with Shapely and Node with mapshaper.
  - `regions.py` defines the informal Old Toronto areas on the City's older 140-neighbourhood map, then carries them over to the current 158 by largest overlap.
  - `build.py` cleans every source. It drops ramps from the expressway centrelines and merges what's left into named routes. It clips federal ridings to the city's shoreline, matches each ward to its provincial riding, and computes a label point inside each polygon.
  - mapshaper simplifies the shapes so the site loads quickly. All the data comes to about 800 KB.
- **No server:** everything is static files, hosted free on Cloudflare Pages. `.github/workflows/deploy.yml` uploads `docs/` with Wrangler whenever it changes: on a push to main, and when one of the fetch jobs that update published data (closures, What's changing, transit schedules, heritage buildings, collection days, voting places, representatives, fact sheets) finishes. Only changed files are sent, and a newer deploy replaces one still running, so a burst of commits publishes once.
- **A page per place:** `engine/places.py <city>` (run by run.sh after assemble) writes a light static page for each of Toronto's 158 neighbourhoods and Chicago's 77 community areas (`/toronto/annex/`, `/chicago/pilsen/`…), with its own preview image, an index page per city, and the site's `sitemap.xml` and `robots.txt`, so search engines can find places and shared links preview well. Each page says in plain text what the card says (where it sits, names used there, what stands out, Living here with the census caveats, neighbours) and its button opens the map focused on the place. Unlisted cities get none.
- **Routes from here (unlisted, Toronto):** `/toronto/routes/` answers "what buses stop near me, and where do they go?" Find your location (it never leaves the browser) or search an address, and the page lists the closest stop for each bus and direction, both sides of the street, with where each one ends and the subway stations it connects to. Pick one and you get a paragraph describing the whole route end to end (where it starts, main streets, the places it serves, subway connections, landmarks, where it ends), then the ride from your stop, in order: the regions and neighbourhoods it passes through, the main streets it runs along and crosses, subway connections, landmarks (malls, hospitals, campuses, bridges) and where it ends, beside a map drawn from the map's own layers. `engine/routes/build.py` precomputes each route pattern's story from the TTC schedule the map already fetches; the page slices it from the stop you picked. Toronto's settings, extra landmarks and wording are in `cities/toronto/routes.json` (buses for now; streetcars are one setting away). Rebuilt with the fact sheets.
- **Fact sheets:** `/toronto/profiles/` and `/nyc/profiles/` are printable, one-page (US Letter) sheets, one for each of Toronto's 158 neighbourhoods and New York's 59 community districts (and its big parks and airports), linked from the hub and every Guide page, in three versions: *Overview* (a large map, at a glance, getting around, parks, learning and community, daily life), *Transit* (stations with TTC line bullets, the routes stopping inside as TTC-style badges, stops and changes to the main hubs, a system map) and *Driving* (nearest on-ramp to each highway, free-flow drive times to Union, Pearson, Yorkdale, Scarborough Town Centre and Square One, Green P, permit parking and EV charging). Facilities only, never demographics. Drop a pin or search an address and the sheet answers from that spot: walk times to the nearest station, stop, Bike Share dock, park and library, and drive times from there. Nothing is computed on a server: `engine/profiles/build.py` writes one small JSON per neighbourhood from the map's own files; `walk_grid.py` precomputes walk times along OpenStreetMap streets and paths (to the nearest subway entrance or park edge) for every 150 m square of the city, so a pin is a lookup, and nothing about it leaves the browser; `fetch_drive.py` reads highway slip roads from OpenStreetMap and drive times from OSRM. Everything a sheet says that is one city's own (its hubs, agencies, units, wording and badge colours) lives in `cities/<city>/factsheet.json` and `factsheet.css`; the page is `engine/profiles/page.html`. New York's stops and changes are counted along each service's midday stops from the MTA schedule, so an express skips what it skips. `.github/workflows/refresh-fact-sheets.yml` rebuilds the sheets whenever routes, parking or bike lanes change, and re-fetches walk and drive times monthly.
- **Compare:** `/compare/` puts any two cities (three on wider screens) side by side, or any of their neighbourhoods, from each city's published census profile file: home value, rent, commuting, transit time to downtown, home types, building age, language at home, recent immigrants, ages and tree canopy. City figures are weighted by each unit's population or households (`pop`, `hh` in the profiles files), with a switch to the unweighted typical-neighbourhood view. In neighbourhood mode, a track under each housing, commuting and canopy figure shows where the place sits among its own city's units, and "Most like" suggests the three closest places in another city: prices compared by rank within each city (markets differ), commuting, canopy, home types and minutes to downtown compared as the figures themselves (they mean the same thing anywhere), building age by rank. It never matches or ranks on ages, languages or who lives there. Each map's card links to it ("Places like … in other cities"). `engine/compare.py` writes the city summaries; matching runs in the browser.
- **Venues:** `/toronto/venues/` lists Toronto's big live venues, from Rogers Stadium and Rogers Centre to Massey Hall, with what's on at each in the coming two weeks and how to get there: nearest subway and GO station with walk times, the streetcars and buses within a five-minute walk, Green P within a ten-minute walk with capacities, hand-written arrival notes from the venues' own pages, and a table of drive time, taxi-meter estimate and transit route from across the GTA, with the GO fare for each trip from GO’s own station-to-station fares in its GTFS (`cities/toronto/fetch_go_fares.py`, monthly) (a "From …" picker on the list page). A "busy nights" strip flags days when two or more big venues are on at once. Listings come from Ticketmaster's Discovery API nightly (`engine/fetch_venue_events.py`), and only the facts are kept: name, date, time, kind and a link to the event's own page. No images, prices, copy or affiliate links; it's a listing, not a guide. Each venue has a permanent page and its own preview image. `engine/venues.py` builds the pages from `cities/toronto/venues.json` and the map's published files; `.github/workflows/refresh-venues.yml` runs it after the nightly fetch. New York has the same pages at `/nyc/venues/` (October 19): 17 venues from MetLife Stadium and Yankee Stadium to Carnegie Hall, including MetLife, Prudential Center and UBS Arena across the city line, in miles, with the subway lines that run straight from Penn Station, Grand Central, the PATH or an origin to the venue's station. Its events are fetched venue by venue (a sweep of New York stops at the API's 1,000-event limit). One engine serves both: each city's words, map files, fares and What's here rows live in its `venues.json`.
- **Report a problem:** a link at the foot of every card opens a small box (what's wrong, an unticked "include this place" box) and then the visitor's own email app, addressed to go@layers.city. No form service and nothing sent by the page; the place goes in only if they tick the box. Config: `card.report` in each city.json.
- **A daily freshness check:** `engine/health.py`, run each day by `.github/workflows/check-feeds.yml`, checks every live file's date (closures daily, What's changing weekly, schedule feeds before they run out, this year's collection and sweeping calendars) and the latest run of every scheduled fetch job. When something is stale or a job failed, it opens one GitHub issue labelled `stale-data` that mentions the owner, so GitHub emails him; it comments when more goes stale and closes the issue once everything is fine again.
- **Visit counts without tracking:** `docs/stats.js`, loaded by the hub and every map, sends visits and feature names ("view: transit", "layer_on: tram", "image: portrait") to the free tier of [Umami Cloud](https://umami.is), cookieless. Before anything leaves the browser it strips the address to its path and keeps only short ids in event data, so pins, place names, searches and location never go out. It stays off with Global Privacy Control, an opt-out on the privacy page, off `layers.city`, or while its site ID is empty. The engine logs events with `logEvent()` at the buttons people press, so the tour, Views and shared links replaying the same functions aren't counted twice.

## Built with AI

I built this with Claude (Anthropic) as the implementation partner. I owned the product side: the problem, which layers matter and how Toronto's informal geography should be described. I also reviewed every output against what I know of the city. Claude handled most of the engineering:

- finding and pulling the official datasets
- writing the geometry pipeline and the front end
- testing the page at desktop and phone sizes

The work was data sourcing as much as it was code. Some examples of the judgment calls involved:

- Federal riding lines changed in 2023, so Toronto now has 24 federal ridings but still 25 provincial ridings. The provincial ridings still match the wards exactly, so they share the wards' official geometry.
- The City's riding layer turned out to hold pre-2018 boundaries. The current ridings come from Elections Canada and Elections Ontario data instead, via Open North.
- There is no official dataset for names like King West or Little Italy. Those are curated by hand and labelled as approximate.

## Known limitations

- Known-as names are approximate centre points, not areas.
- Federal riding shapes are simplified, so a point within a few metres of a riding edge can be misattributed. Wards and provincial ridings use the City's detailed lines.
- There's no detailed street basemap: the map draws main streets, highways and transit, but not every local street. That's on purpose, to keep it readable; for street-level detail, use Google Maps or OpenStreetMap.
- The neighbourhood lens uses the 2021 Census, so prices are a few years old, and commuting was counted in May 2021, during the pandemic, when transit use was unusually low. The tiers are relative to the rest of Toronto, which is what they're meant to show.
- Times to Union come from published schedules, not real-world delays, and are for one destination. There's no driving time on purpose: free routing tools assume empty roads, which badly understates a Toronto rush hour.
- Special-event closure times are the City's permit times, a day's snapshot; roads often reopen sooner, once the last runners pass. The course is drawn from the closures themselves (organizers don't publish their course as open data), so a stretch that isn't closed, like a path through a park, isn't drawn.
- Road closures are a daily snapshot of permits, not live traffic: work can finish early, run late or be renewed, and utility work done for a transit project but filed by the utility (Toronto Hydro, Enbridge) isn't included. Lines under construction show only once OpenStreetMap maps them (Yonge North isn't yet).
- Representatives are refreshed weekly, so for a few days after an election or by-election the card can lag. Vacant seats say so.
- Address matching depends on OpenStreetMap's address coverage, which is good in Toronto but not complete. The free Nominatim service also asks for no more than one search per second, which the page enforces.
- Chicago: the sides are a convention, not an official boundary, and the main streets come from OpenStreetMap plus a hand-kept list of the mile-grid arterials and diagonals. Times to the Loop come from published schedules, like Toronto's times to Union.

## Data sources and licences

- City of Toronto Open Data, under the [Open Government Licence – Toronto](https://open.toronto.ca/open-data-license/): former municipalities, wards, neighbourhoods, Neighbourhood Profiles (2021 Census), BIAs, Heritage Conservation Districts, expressway centrelines and the TTC GTFS feed.
- Elected representatives from [toronto.ca](https://www.toronto.ca/city-government/council/members-of-council/), the [Legislative Assembly of Ontario](https://www.ola.org/en/members/current) and the [House of Commons](https://www.ourcommons.ca/members/en).
- Electoral boundaries from Elections Canada (2023 Representation Order) and Elections Ontario, via [Open North Represent](https://represent.opennorth.ca/).
- Subway, LRT and GO/UP geometry derived from TTC and Metrolinx GTFS via [agcghub/toronto-bus-map](https://github.com/Miqell24/toronto-bus-map).
- Voting subdivisions and voting places for the 2026 municipal election from the City of Toronto's [Voting Places & Ward Maps](https://www.toronto.ca/city-government/elections/city-elections/voting-places/) page (its ArcGIS Online layers and the All Wards list). No licence is stated on these files; re-check before any commercial use.
- Heritage buildings: City of Toronto Heritage Register (Part IV designations) and City of Chicago [Individual Landmarks](https://data.cityofchicago.org/Historic-Preservation/Individual-Landmarks/uct4-hrvh).
- Development applications, building permits and address points: City of Toronto Open Data (Open Government Licence – Toronto); building permits: [City of Chicago Data Portal](https://data.cityofchicago.org/Buildings/Building-Permits/ydr8-5enu).
- Bike lanes: City of Toronto Open Data [Cycling Network](https://open.toronto.ca/dataset/cycling-network/) and City of Chicago [Bike Routes](https://data.cityofchicago.org/d/hvv9-38ut) (CDOT). Neither dataset states its own licence; they fall under each portal's terms, so re-check before any commercial use.
- Bike Share stations: Bike Share Toronto's [GBFS feed](https://tor.publicbikesystem.net/ube/gbfs/v1/gbfs.json) (Toronto Parking Authority), linked from the City's [open data portal](https://open.toronto.ca/dataset/bike-share-toronto/), which states no licence: re-check before any commercial use.
- Parking: City of Toronto Open Data [Green P Parking](https://open.toronto.ca/dataset/green-p-parking/) (2019, not refreshed), [On-Street Permit Parking Area Maps](https://open.toronto.ca/dataset/on-street-permit-parking-area-maps/) and [City Operated Electric Vehicle Charging Stations](https://open.toronto.ca/dataset/city-operated-electric-vehicle-charging-station-map/) (no licence stated on the packages: re-check before any commercial use); EV charging stations from the [Alternative Fuels Data Center](https://afdc.energy.gov/) station API (U.S. Department of Energy), the listings behind Natural Resources Canada's station locator (and New York's chargers). New York's municipal garages and fields from NYC DOT's [list of municipal parking facilities](https://www.nyc.gov/html/dot/html/motorist/parkinglist.shtml), placed with NYC Planning's GeoSearch and OpenStreetMap intersections.
- Tree cover from [ESA WorldCover 2021](https://esa-worldcover.org) (10 m, CC BY 4.0), both cities.
- GO bus routes and stops from Metrolinx's GO GTFS ([Metrolinx Open Data](https://www.metrolinx.com/en/about-us/open-data), Open Government Licence – Ontario – Metrolinx).
- Travel times to Union computed from the TTC schedules and from Metrolinx's GO and UP Express GTFS ([Metrolinx Open Data](https://www.metrolinx.com/en/about-us/open-data)), with walking routes from OpenStreetMap.
- Suburban drive times (both cities) routed with [OSRM](https://project-osrm.org/) on © [OpenStreetMap](https://www.openstreetmap.org/copyright) contributors' roads (ODbL).
- Highways outside Toronto © [OpenStreetMap](https://www.openstreetmap.org/copyright) contributors (ODbL), fetched with the Overpass API.
- Skyscrapers (both cities) from Wikipedia's "List of tallest buildings in Toronto" and "…in Chicago" ([CC BY-SA 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/)); addresses of towers under construction placed with OpenStreetMap's Nominatim.
- Road closures and special-event closures from the City of Toronto's [Road Restrictions](https://open.toronto.ca/dataset/road-restrictions/) live feed (Open Government Licence – Toronto); lines under construction © [OpenStreetMap](https://www.openstreetmap.org/copyright) contributors (ODbL).
- Neighbouring municipalities © [OpenStreetMap](https://www.openstreetmap.org/copyright) contributors (ODbL). Lake Ontario, Lake Simcoe and Lake Scugog shorelines around the GTA from OpenStreetMap; lakes farther out from [Natural Earth](https://www.naturalearthdata.com/) (public domain).
